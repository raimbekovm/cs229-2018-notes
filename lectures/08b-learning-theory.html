Based on the Friday discussion section of CS229, Stanford University, Autumn 2018, taught by a member of the course staff.

Lecture 8 told you to choose models on held-out data because training error is optimistic. This section asks how optimistic, and answers with a theorem: if an algorithm picks the hypothesis with the lowest training error from a class of kkk hypotheses, then with probability at least 1−δ1 - \delta1−δ its true error is within

212mlog⁡2kδ 2\sqrt{\frac{1}{2m}\log\frac{2k}{\delta}} 22m1​logδ2k​​

of the best the class can do. Two facts are hiding in that expression. The number of examples needed grows only with the logarithm of the size of the class, and, once the class is infinite, the logarithm is replaced by a measure of the class’s expressiveness called the VC dimension. Getting there takes two elementary tools — the union bound and Hoeffding’s inequality — and a change of viewpoint about what a learning algorithm is.

Ng explained in lecture 8 that this material used to be part of the main lectures and moved to a Friday section because it matters less to making algorithms work than it once did. The section promises to state results rather than prove them, and the proofs are in cs229-notes4. What this chapter adds is the section’s own pictures, its answers to questions, and a computation behind every claim: the bounds are checked against exact probabilities and simulations, which also shows how loose they are.

The learning algorithm as an estimator

1:15 Everything rests on two assumptions. There is a fixed data distribution D\mathcal DD over pairs (x,y)(x, y)(x,y), from which both the training set and every future test example are drawn; and the examples are drawn independently. Without the first, training data say nothing about test data, and the theory has nothing to work with. Lecture 8’s aside about training on phone recordings to deploy on a smart speaker is exactly a case where it fails.

3:15 A learning algorithm is then a box. In goes a training set S={(x(i),y(i))}i=1mS = \{(x^{(i)}, y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^mS={(x(i),y(i))}i=1m​ of mmm samples from D\mathcal DD; out comes a hypothesis h^\hat hh^, or equivalently, for a parametric class such as logistic regression in nnn dimensions, a parameter vector θ^\hat\thetaθ^. 4:43 The point the section makes first is that SSS is random, the box is a deterministic function, and so its output is random too. Run the box on a different sample from D\mathcal DD and you get a different θ^\hat\thetaθ^. In statistics the box is called an estimator, and the distribution of θ^\hat\thetaθ^ over repeated draws of SSS is its sampling distribution.

6:22 Against this randomness stands a fixed target, a true parameter θ∗\theta^*θ∗ (or true hypothesis h∗h^*h∗) that we would like the box to output. It is unknown, but it is not random: there is no distribution over it. That is the frequentist stance of lecture 8, and the notation follows it — hats for things estimated from data, stars for the true values we never see. Asked whether θ\thetaθ must be a vector, the section answers that it can be anything we estimate, a scalar, a matrix, or a function with no parameters at all.

Bias and variance as moments of the sampling distribution

10:26 Lecture 8 pictured bias and variance in the data view: curves through points, underfitting and overfitting. The section redraws them in the parameter view, which makes them precise. Take a model with two parameters, so that each θ^\hat\thetaθ^ is a dot in the plane. Draw a training set of size mmm, run the algorithm, plot the dot; draw another training set of the same size, plot another dot; repeat. The cloud of dots is a sample from the sampling distribution. 13:51 Bias asks whether the cloud is centred on θ∗\theta^*θ∗: it is E[θ^]−θ∗\mathbb E[\hat\theta] - \theta^*E[θ^]−θ∗, a statement about the first moment. Variance asks how spread out the cloud is, the second moment. They are, in the section’s words, “just properties of the first and second moments of your sampling distribution”.

Least squares is unbiased, and at m=10m = 10m=10 its cloud is centred on θ∗\theta^*θ∗ but enormous (total variance 1.471.471.47), stretched along the direction in which correlated features cannot be told apart: raising θ1\theta_1θ1​ while lowering θ2\theta_2θ2​ barely changes the fit. Ridge regression with λ=10\lambda = 10λ=10 on the same training sets is pulled towards the origin, a bias of length 0.520.520.52, and its variance is thirty times smaller, 0.0450.0450.045. That is lecture 8’s claim that regularisation trades a little bias for a lot of variance, seen directly.

15:58 With ten times the data, every cloud tightens: least squares’ variance falls from 1.471.471.47 to 0.1100.1100.110, roughly the factor of ten one expects from variance proportional to 1/m1/m1/m. As m→∞m \to \inftym→∞ the variance goes to zero, and an estimator whose output converges to θ∗\theta^*θ∗ is called consistent. 17:56 An estimator with E[θ^]=θ∗\mathbb E[\hat\theta] = \theta^*E[θ^]=θ∗ at every mmm is called unbiased.

17:14 The rate at which variance falls with mmm the section calls statistical efficiency, a measure of how well an algorithm squeezes information from data, and it offers as examples one algorithm whose variance falls like 1/m21/m^21/m2 and another like e−me^{-m}e−m. Those rates are not available to an unbiased estimator of a parameter in a regular model: the Cramér–Rao bound puts a floor of order 1/m1/m1/m under its variance. (Irregular models are different — the right endpoint of a uniform distribution can be estimated with variance of order 1/m21/m^21/m2 — but the models in this course are regular.) What efficiency usually measures is the constant in front of 1/m1/m1/m. The mean and the median of a Gaussian sample are both unbiased estimates of its centre, both with variance proportional to 1/m1/m1/m; but the median’s is larger by a factor of π/2\pi/2π/2. The generator measures m⋅Var⁡m \cdot \operatorname{Var}m⋅Var at 1.011.011.01 for the mean and 1.5751.5751.575 for the median at m=101m = 101m=101, against π/2=1.571\pi/2 = 1.571π/2=1.571. Using the median wastes about a third of the data.

20:08 Informally, the section says, a high-bias algorithm is one that “no matter how much data or evidence you provide”, stays away from θ∗\theta^*θ∗. That runs two ideas together, and the bottom row of Figure 1 separates them. Bias is defined at a fixed mmm, as a student’s question prompts the section to confirm at 21:35; staying away forever is inconsistency. Ridge with a fixed λ\lambdaλ is biased at every mmm and yet consistent, because the penalty stays the same size while the data term grows in proportion to mmm: its bias is 0.520.520.52 at m=10m = 10m=10, 0.180.180.18 at 100100100, 0.0300.0300.030 at 1,0001{,}0001,000 and 0.0020.0020.002 at 10,00010{,}00010,000. 58:09 The same point answers a later question that the section leaves open, whether adding regularisation destroys the consistency of maximum likelihood. With a fixed penalty, it does not.

20:58 Bias and variance are separate properties; any combination of the two can occur. 22:18 High variance has two standard remedies. More data shrinks the cloud directly. Regularisation shrinks it by restricting where θ^\hat\thetaθ^ can go, usually at the price of moving its centre — which is the trade Figure 1 shows.

Approximation error and estimation error

25:02 The second picture is drawn in the space of all hypotheses. Somewhere in it is ggg, the best possible hypothesis, with the lowest expected error under D\mathcal DD of any function whatsoever. 25:52 Our algorithm searches only a class H\mathcal HH — all logistic regression models, say, or all SVMs — drawn as a region that need not contain ggg. 26:27 Inside the region is h∗h^*h∗, the best hypothesis in the class. And what training on finite data actually produces is h^\hat hh^, a different point of H\mathcal HH for every training set.

27:51 For classification the error of a hypothesis is its probability of a mistake, and the training error is its frequency of mistakes on SSS:

31:35 The identity is a telescoping sum, but each term names a different cause. Approximation error is the price of restricting ourselves to H\mathcal HH, a property of the class alone. 32:20 Estimation error is the price of having only mmm examples, plus any quirks of the algorithm, and it shrinks as mmm grows.

34:49 The section then says this decomposition and the bias–variance decomposition are related but not the same: estimation error itself splits into a variance part and a bias part, and “bias” in the usual sense is approximation error plus that estimation bias. 36:34 The picture also says how to fight each kind of error. Empirical risk minimisation and two tools

44:56 Nothing so far depended on what happens inside the box. To prove something, the section now fixes the algorithm: empirical risk minimisation returns the hypothesis with the lowest training error,

h^=arg⁡min⁡h∈Hε^S(h).(4) \hat h = \arg\min_{h \in \mathcal H} \hat\varepsilon_S(h). \tag{4}h^=argh∈Hmin​ε^S​(h).(4)

47:36 This is “what we’ve been doing so far in the course”, with one honest caveat that the section mentions: logistic regression minimises the logistic loss, not the 0–1 training error of Equation 1, and SVMs minimise the hinge loss. Those losses are convex stand-ins for the count of mistakes, which is intractable to minimise directly. The theorems below are about the count of mistakes; they extend to the stand-ins with more work, which this section does not do.

49:25 Two questions organise the rest. How close is the training error of a hypothesis to its true error? And how close is the true error of the hypothesis ERM picks to that of the best in the class? 50:47 The first tool is the union bound: for any events A1,…,AkA_1, \dots, A_kA1​,…,Ak​, independent or not,

P(A1∪⋯∪Ak)≤P(A1)+⋯+P(Ak). P(A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_k) \le P(A_1) + \dots + P(A_k). P(A1​∪⋯∪Ak​)≤P(A1​)+⋯+P(Ak​).

“If this looks trivial, it is trivial,” the section says. 52:00 The second is Hoeffding’s inequality, stated for the case needed here. Let Z1,…,ZmZ_1, \dots, Z_mZ1​,…,Zm​ be independent Bernoulli(ϕ\phiϕ) variables and ϕ^=1m∑iZi\hat\phi = \frac1m\sum_i Z_iϕ^​=m1​∑i​Zi​ their mean. Then for any γ>0\gamma > 0γ>0,

P(∣ϕ^−ϕ∣>γ)≤2exp⁡(−2γ2m).(5) P\bigl(|\hat\phi - \phi| > \gamma\bigr) \le 2\exp(-2\gamma^2 m). \tag{5}P(∣ϕ^​−ϕ∣>γ)≤2exp(−2γ2m).(5)

The probability that an average of coin flips misses its expectation by more than a margin γ\gammaγ falls exponentially in the number of flips. The general version holds for any bounded independent variables, and the bound on the range is essential.

The bound is easy to check, because the exact probability is a binomial tail. Figure 3 compares them for γ=0.1\gamma = 0.1γ=0.1. At m=100m = 100m=100 the exact probabilities are 0.0350.0350.035 for a fair coin and 0.00340.00340.0034 for ϕ=0.15\phi = 0.15ϕ=0.15, against a bound of 0.270.270.27. The bound holds at every mmm and ϕ\phiϕ the generator tries, with the exact tail never more than 0.230.230.23 of it. It has the right exponential rate, and it is loosest for coins far from fair, whose averages vary less — a looseness that matters here, because error rates of good classifiers are far from one half.

Uniform convergence for a finite class

59:54 The section’s central picture puts every hypothesis in H\mathcal HH on a horizontal axis and plots two curves above it: the true error ε(h)\varepsilon(h)ε(h), a fixed curve, and the training error ε^S(h)\hat\varepsilon_S(h)ε^S​(h), a random curve that changes with every training set. Figure 4 computes it for a class small enough that the true error of every member is a closed form. Inputs are uniform on [0,1][0,1][0,1], the label is 1{x≥0.4}1\{x \ge 0.4\}1{x≥0.4} flipped with probability 0.150.150.15, and H\mathcal HH contains ten threshold classifiers ht(x)=1{x≥t}h_t(x) = 1\{x \ge t\}ht​(x)=1{x≥t} with t=0.05,0.15,…,0.95t = 0.05, 0.15, \dots, 0.95t=0.05,0.15,…,0.95. The Bayes error is 0.150.150.15. The class has no threshold at 0.40.40.4, so its best members, t=0.35t = 0.35t=0.35 and t=0.45t = 0.45t=0.45, have error 0.1850.1850.185, an approximation error of 0.0350.0350.035.

1:05:19 For one hypothesis hhh fixed in advance, the training error is an average of mmm independent Bernoulli variables 1{h(x(i))≠y(i)}1\{h(x^{(i)}) \ne y^{(i)}\}1{h(x(i))=y(i)} whose mean is ε(h)\varepsilon(h)ε(h), so Hoeffding applies directly:

P(∣ε^S(h)−ε(h)∣>γ)≤2exp⁡(−2γ2m). P\bigl(|\hat\varepsilon_S(h) - \varepsilon(h)| > \gamma\bigr) \le 2\exp(-2\gamma^2 m). P(∣ε^S​(h)−ε(h)∣>γ)≤2exp(−2γ2m).

On average over training sets the dashed curve passes through the solid one at every hhh, and with more data it hugs it more tightly. 1:08:16 But this is useless as it stands, and the section is precise about why: ERM does not fix hhh in advance. It looks at the training set and picks the hypothesis whose dashed curve is lowest, so h^\hat hh^ depends on SSS and its training error is no longer an unbiased average. It is the minimum of noisy scores, which lecture 8 showed is optimistic. The circled point in Figure 4 is exactly that.

1:09:32 The fix is to demand that the whole dashed curve be close to the solid one at once — uniform convergence. If no hypothesis deviates by more than γ\gammaγ, then neither does the one ERM picks, however it was chosen. 1:11:54 For a class of kkk hypotheses the union bound gives this cheaply: the event that some hhh deviates is the union of kkk events, each of probability at most 2exp⁡(−2γ2m)2\exp(-2\gamma^2 m)2exp(−2γ2m), so

P(∃ h∈H:∣ε^S(h)−ε(h)∣>γ)≤2kexp⁡(−2γ2m).(6) P\bigl(\exists\, h \in \mathcal H : |\hat\varepsilon_S(h) - \varepsilon(h)| > \gamma\bigr) \le 2k\exp(-2\gamma^2 m). \tag{6}P(∃h∈H:∣ε^S​(h)−ε(h)∣>γ)≤2kexp(−2γ2m).(6)

Equivalently, with probability at least 1−δ1 - \delta1−δ, where δ=2kexp⁡(−2γ2m)\delta = 2k \exp(-2\gamma^2 m)δ=2kexp(−2γ2m), every hypothesis has training error within γ\gammaγ of its true error.

The union bound is pessimistic, since it treats the kkk events as if they could not overlap, and nested thresholds overlap heavily. With m=200m = 200m=200 and γ=0.1\gamma = 0.1γ=0.1 the generator finds that some hypothesis among the ten deviates in 1.6 %1.6\,\%1.6% of training sets. The bound for a single hypothesis is already 3.7 %3.7\,\%3.7%, and the union bound is 37 %37\,\%37%. The guarantees in learning theory are worst-case statements over every distribution, and they read best as statements about how quantities scale.

1:16:04 Three quantities are tied together — mmm, γ\gammaγ and δ\deltaδ — and fixing any two gives the third. Fixing γ\gammaγ and δ\deltaδ and solving for mmm gives the sample complexity: with probability at least 1−δ1 - \delta1−δ, every training error is within γ\gammaγ of the true error, provided

m≥12γ2log⁡2kδ.(7) m \ge \frac{1}{2\gamma^2}\log\frac{2k}{\delta}. \tag{7}m≥2γ21​logδ2k​.(7)

The striking feature is the logarithm. For γ=δ=0.05\gamma = \delta = 0.05γ=δ=0.05, ten hypotheses need 1,1991{,}1991,199 examples, a thousand need 2,1202{,}1202,120, and a million need only 3,5013{,}5013,501.

How good is the hypothesis ERM picks?

1:17:58 The second question compares h^\hat hh^ with the best in class. On the event that uniform convergence holds, a three-step chain does it, each step either uniform convergence or the definition of ERM:

ε(h^)≤ε^S(h^)+γ≤ε^S(h∗)+γ≤ε(h∗)+2γ. \varepsilon(\hat h) \le \hat\varepsilon_S(\hat h) + \gamma \le \hat\varepsilon_S(h^*) + \gamma \le \varepsilon(h^*) + 2\gamma . ε(h^)≤ε^S​(h^)+γ≤ε^S​(h∗)+γ≤ε(h∗)+2γ.

The first step drops from the solid curve to the dashed one at h^\hat hh^. The second uses the fact that h^\hat hh^ has the lowest training error of any hypothesis, h∗h^*h∗ included. The third climbs from the dashed curve back to the solid one at h∗h^*h∗. 1:22:29 Substituting γ\gammaγ from Equation 6 gives the theorem: with probability at least 1−δ1 - \delta1−δ,

ε(h^)≤(min⁡h∈Hε(h))+212mlog⁡2kδ.(8) \varepsilon(\hat h) \le \Bigl(\min_{h \in \mathcal H} \varepsilon(h)\Bigr) + 2\sqrt{\frac{1}{2m}\log\frac{2k}{\delta}} . \tag{8}ε(h^)≤(h∈Hmin​ε(h))+22m1​logδ2k​​.(8)

This is bias and variance once more, in theorem form. The first term is the best the class can do, which can only fall as H\mathcal HH grows; the second, which grows with kkk, is the price of searching a larger class with the same data. cs229-notes4 makes the same reading. On the threshold class the guarantee holds in every one of 3,000 runs at each mmm the generator tries, but it is very loose: at m=200m = 200m=200 and δ=0.05\delta = 0.05δ=0.05, 2γ2\gamma2γ is 0.2450.2450.245, while ERM picks a best-in-class threshold in every run.

Infinite classes and the VC dimension

1:23:40 Most classes of interest, linear classifiers among them, are infinite, and Equation 8 with k=∞k = \inftyk=∞ says nothing. The section ran out of time here and only stated the result; cs229-notes4, section 4, gives more, and two pieces of it are worth having.

The first is a crude argument that gets the right answer. A class with ddd real parameters, stored as 64-bit floating-point numbers, has at most k=264dk = 2^{64d}k=264d members, and log⁡k=64dlog⁡2\log k = 64 d \log 2logk=64dlog2 is linear in ddd. So the sample complexity of ERM over a ddd-parameter class grows linearly in ddd. With γ=δ=0.05\gamma = \delta = 0.05γ=δ=0.05 the bound gives about 9,6009{,}6009,600 examples for d=1d = 1d=1 and 89,00089{,}00089,000 for d=10d = 10d=10, which is far too many in absolute terms and right in how it scales.

The second is the principled version. A class H\mathcal HH shatters a set of points if, for every one of the 2n2^n2n ways of labelling them, some h∈Hh \in \mathcal Hh∈H gets every label right. The Vapnik–Chervonenkis dimension VC(H)\mathrm{VC}(\mathcal H)VC(H) is the size of the largest set H\mathcal HH can shatter. For lines in the plane it is 3, and Figure 5 shows why: three points in general position can be split by a line under all eight labellings, while no four points can. Any four points in general position either form a convex quadrilateral, where the labelling that alternates around it defeats every line, or a triangle with a point inside, where labelling the inner point against the outer three does. The generator checks every labelling of each set by exhaustive search over line directions. The definition needs only one shattered set of size 3: three collinear points cannot be shattered, since no line puts the middle point on the other side from its two neighbours, and the VC dimension is still 3.

1:24:52 Vapnik’s theorem then replaces log⁡k\log klogk with the VC dimension ddd: with probability at least 1−δ1 - \delta1−δ,

ε(h^)≤ε(h∗)+O(dmlog⁡md+1mlog⁡1δ), \varepsilon(\hat h) \le \varepsilon(h^*) + O\Bigl(\sqrt{\frac{d}{m}\log\frac{m}{d} + \frac1m\log\frac1\delta}\Bigr), ε(h^)≤ε(h∗)+O(md​logdm​+m1​logδ1​​),

and the section’s take-away is that the number of examples needed is of the order of the VC dimension. For most classes with a reasonable parametrisation the VC dimension is roughly the number of parameters — linear classifiers in nnn dimensions have n+1n + 1n+1 — which is the formal version of Ng’s rule of thumb in lecture 8 that unregularised models want at least as many examples as parameters.

The VC dimension also answers the question from lecture 8 about why an SVM in an infinite-dimensional feature space does not overfit. The VC dimension of all linear separators in that space is infinite. But Ng’s answer was that the relevant class is the separators with a large margin, and for data inside a ball of radius RRR the VC dimension of separators with margin at least γ\gammaγ is bounded in terms of R2/γ2R^2/\gamma^2R2/γ2, however many dimensions the feature space has. That is the sense in which minimising ∥w∥2\lVert w \rVert^2∥w∥2 is regularisation.

7  Kernels

Lecture 7

Keywords

kernel trick, support vector machines, Lagrange duality, representer theorem, Mercer’s theorem, soft margin SVM

Lecture video: Lecture 7: Kernels · 1:20:25

Based on the lecture by Andrew Ng — CS229, Stanford University, Autumn 2018.

A support vector machine is the optimal margin classifier of lecture 6 run in a feature space of enormous, possibly infinite, dimension. That sounds impossible to compute, and the lecture’s argument is a chain of three observations that make it cheap. The optimal www is always a weighted sum of the training examples. Once www is written that way, the training problem and the prediction rule touch the data only through inner products ⟨x(i),x(j)⟩\langle x^{(i)}, x^{(j)} \rangle⟨x(i),x(j)⟩. And for some very large feature maps ϕ\phiϕ, the inner product ϕ(x)⊤ϕ(z)\phi(x)^\top \phi(z)ϕ(x)⊤ϕ(z) can be computed directly from xxx and zzz in time proportional to their own dimension — that shortcut is a kernel. Ng calls the SVM one of his favourite algorithms because it is “very turnkey”: the optimisation is convex and good packages solve it. The lecture ends by dropping the assumption that the data are separable and with kernels designed for inputs that are not vectors at all.

7.1 From the largest margin to the smallest www

1:13 Lecture 6 posed the optimal margin classifier as: choose γ\gammaγ, www and bbb to maximise γ\gammaγ, subject to every example having geometric margin at least γ\gammaγ. 5:31 Ng spends a minute on why that maximises the worst margin. If three examples had geometric margins 171717, 222 and 555, the set’s margin is 222, and the only way to raise γ\gammaγ under the constraints is to raise the margin of whichever example is lowest. Pushing γ\gammaγ up as far as it goes is the same as maximising min⁡iγ(i)\min_i \gamma^{(i)}mini​γ(i).

6:33 The step to a tractable problem uses the freedom that made the functional margin useless in lecture 6. Take w=(2,1)w = (2, 1)w=(2,1) and b=−2b = -2b=−2: the boundary 2x1+x2=22x_1 + x_2 = 22x1​+x2​=2 crosses the axes at (1,0)(1, 0)(1,0) and (0,2)(0, 2)(0,2), and so does the boundary for 10w10w10w and 10b10b10b, or for w/10w/10w/10 and b/10b/10b/10. Because rescaling (w,b)(w, b)(w,b) changes nothing about the classifier, we may pick the scale, and 8:49 the convenient choice is the one that makes the worst functional margin exactly 111: min⁡iy(i)(w⊤x(i)+b)=1\min_i y^{(i)}(w^\top x^{(i)} + b) = 1mini​y(i)(w⊤x(i)+b)=1. 7.2 The representer theorem

10:34 So far xxx has been two- or a hundred-dimensional. The goal is feature vectors with a hundred trillion dimensions, or infinitely many, where www cannot even be stored. 11:07 The way through is to assume that www is a linear combination of the training examples,

w=∑i=1mαi y(i)x(i),(7.2) w = \sum_{i=1}^m \alpha_i \, y^{(i)} x^{(i)} , \tag{7.2}w=i=1∑m​αi​y(i)x(i),(7.2)

with the labels y(i)=±1y^{(i)} = \pm 1y(i)=±1 written in by convention because they make later algebra tidier. 11:57 Ng presents this as an assumption but stresses that it is not one: the representer theorem proves that the optimal www always has this form, so nothing is lost. He skips the proof as long and involved and gives two intuitions.

13:11 The first is from gradient descent. Run stochastic gradient descent for logistic regression from θ=0\theta = 0θ=0. Each update adds a multiple of one training example, θ:=θ+α (y(i)−hθ(x(i))) x(i)\theta := \theta + \alpha \, (y^{(i)} - h_\theta(x^{(i)}))\, x^{(i)}θ:=θ+α(y(i)−hθ​(x(i)))x(i) (this α\alphaα is the learning rate, unrelated to the αi\alpha_iαi​ above). By induction, θ\thetaθ is a linear combination of training examples after every step, however many steps you take; 15:22 batch gradient descent is the same, and so is gradient descent on the SVM’s own objective. The generator runs 300 SGD steps in five dimensions on three examples and finds θ\thetaθ within 10−1510^{-15}10−15 of the span of those three vectors.

16:09 The second is geometric. 16:50 The vector www is perpendicular to the decision boundary: it fixes the boundary’s orientation, and bbb slides the boundary along it. 18:39 Now suppose the inputs have three features but every training example has x3=0x_3 = 0x3​=0, so the data lie in the x1x2x_1 x_2x1​x2​-plane. Then, Ng says, w3w_3w3​ should be 000 — www should lie in the space the examples span.

That second intuition is worth sharpening, because as stated it is not true of every separator. With all x3=0x_3 = 0x3​=0, the term w3x3w_3 x_3w3​x3​ is zero on every training example, so any value of w3w_3w3​ classifies the training set identically: the generator takes the SVM’s www and sets w3=5w_3 = 5w3​=5, and every margin constraint still holds. What forces w3=0w_3 = 0w3​=0 is the objective. A non-zero w3w_3w3​ adds w32w_3^2w32​ to ∥w∥2\lVert w \rVert^2∥w∥2 and buys nothing in the constraints, so the minimum-norm solution drops it. That is the real content of the representer theorem: among all the www that fit the data equally well, minimising ∥w∥2\lVert w \rVert^2∥w∥2 (or, in general, any loss plus a squared-norm regulariser) picks one in the span of the data.

7.4 The kernel trick

28:42 Ng gives the recipe in four steps. First, write the whole algorithm in terms of inner products ⟨x,z⟩\langle x, z \rangle⟨x,z⟩. Second, choose a feature map ϕ\phiϕ from the original inputs to a higher-dimensional space — 30:12 a house’s floor area xxx could become (x,x2,x3,x4)(x, x^2, x^3, x^4)(x,x2,x3,x4), or area and bedrooms (x1,x2)(x_1, x_2)(x1​,x2​) could become all their products up to some degree. Third, find a way to compute

K(x,z)=ϕ(x)⊤ϕ(z),(7.5) K(x, z) = \phi(x)^\top \phi(z) , \tag{7.5}K(x,z)=ϕ(x)⊤ϕ(z),(7.5)

the kernel function, cheaply. Fourth, replace every ⟨x,z⟩\langle x, z \rangle⟨x,z⟩ in the algorithm by K(x,z)K(x, z)K(x,z). 32:19 The algorithm is then running on ϕ(x)\phi(x)ϕ(x), in the high-dimensional space, without ever forming ϕ(x)\phi(x)ϕ(x). Forming it is what would be expensive: Ng has seen problems with a billion features, and even 100,000 makes every vector operation costly.

33:55 A student asks about the no-free-lunch theorem, and Ng’s answer frames why this works at all. The theorem — Wolpert’s form of it for supervised learning shows that, averaged over all possible target functions, no learning algorithm has lower off-training-set error than any other — says that in the worst case learning does not work. Ng finds it less useful than fascinating, because the problems we meet are not drawn adversarially: “the universe is not that hostile toward us”, and an inductive bias such as “the boundary is smooth in some feature space” tends to pay off.

44:23 This is what Ng means by the SVM: the optimal margin classifier plus the kernel trick. Choose a kernel, solve Equation 7.3 with K(x(i),x(j))K(x^{(i)}, x^{(j)})K(x(i),x(j)) in place of the inner products, and predict with Equation 7.4 likewise; the computation scales with the dimension of xxx, not of ϕ(x)\phi(x)ϕ(x). 45:51 A student asks whether a kernel belongs to one feature map. Essentially yes: up to trivial changes such as permuting the features, which leave every inner product unchanged, a different feature map needs a different kernel.

47:09 To show what this does geometrically, Ng plays a short visualisation by Udi Aharoni. Red and blue points in the plane cannot be separated by a line; a feature map lifts them into three dimensions, where a plane separates them; and the plane, viewed back in the original plane, cuts out an ellipse. 7.7 Which functions are kernels

50:02 Ng’s working intuition is that a kernel measures similarity. An inner product is large when two vectors point the same way and small when they point in unrelated directions, so K(x,z)K(x, z)K(x,z) should be large when xxx and zzz are alike and small when they are not. 51:19 By that standard, here is a function pulled “out of the air”:

K(x,z)=exp⁡ ⁣(−∥x−z∥22σ2),(7.7) K(x, z) = \exp\!\left( -\frac{\lVert x - z \rVert^2}{2\sigma^2} \right) , \tag{7.7}K(x,z)=exp(−2σ2∥x−z∥2​),(7.7)

Ng proves only the direction above and states the other.

The similarity intuition is a good guide but not a test, and the gap is easy to fall into. “Two points are similar if they are within 1.5 of each other” sounds like a reasonable similarity, K(x,z)=1{∣x−z∣<1.5}K(x, z) = 1\{|x - z| < 1.5\}K(x,z)=1{∣x−z∣<1.5}. 7.8 Kernelising other algorithms

1:01:20 The kernel trick is not specific to the SVM. Any algorithm that can be written in terms of inner products of inputs can be kernelised, and Ng lists linear regression, logistic regression, every generalised linear model, the perceptron (which the problem sets kernelise) and, later in the course, principal components analysis. Linear regression in an infinite-dimensional feature space is a perfectly well-defined computation. 1:03:07 In practice, though, the trick is applied overwhelmingly to SVMs and much less often elsewhere.

1:01:26 Ng credits the popularisation to Vladimir Vapnik and Corinna Cortes. The history is slightly split between two papers. The kernelised maximum-margin classifier was introduced by Boser, Guyon and Vapnik in 1992; Cortes and Vapnik’s “Support-vector networks” of 1995 added the soft margin of the next section.

7.9 The soft margin

1:04:07 Mapping to a high-dimensional space makes data much more separable, but a separating boundary is not always wanted. 7.10 Kernels for inputs that are not vectors

1:13:39 In practice the polynomial kernel (x⊤z)d(x^\top z)^d(x⊤z)d works well, and Ng now calls the Gaussian kernel the one most used; earlier he gave that place to the linear kernel, and between them the two are the usual first choices. 1:14:05 One of the early proof points for SVMs was handwritten digit recognition: unroll an image into a vector of pixel intensities and train an SVM on it. On the classic MNIST benchmark this was, in Ng’s words, a very surprising result, and polynomial-kernel SVMs appear beside the convolutional networks of the time in the comparison of classifiers by LeCun et al. 1:15:07 Ng’s summary is that SVMs were for a time the best algorithm on it, with convolutional networks pulling ahead in more recent years.

1:15:25 Much of the inventive work on SVMs, Ng says, has gone into designing kernels, and his example is a protein classifier. A protein is a variable-length sequence of amino acids, of which there are 20 (Ng uses the letters A to Z on the board for convenience). 1:17:01 One feature vector for a sequence counts every possible run of four amino acids — how many times AAAA occurs, then AAAB, and so on to the end of the list — so that ϕ(x)\phi(x)ϕ(x) has 204=160,00020^4 = 160{,}000204=160,000 entries (with Ng’s 26 letters it would be 456,976). 1:18:20 Writing it out is expensive, but K(x,z)=ϕ(x)⊤ϕ(z)K(x, z) = \phi(x)^\top \phi(z)K(x,z)=ϕ(x)⊤ϕ(z) can be computed directly from the two sequences. Ng describes a dynamic programme reminiscent of Knuth–Morris–Pratt string matching; the kernel as published, the spectrum kernel of Leslie, Eskin and Noble, builds a suffix tree and computes it in time linear in the two sequences’ lengths. Only the four-letter runs that actually occur contribute, so a dictionary of counts per sequence is already enough, and the generator confirms that this gives the same number as the explicit 160,000-dimensional inner product.

1:19:35 If the inputs are histograms — the demographic make-up of two countries, say — one kernel sums the bin-by-bin minimum of the two histograms, K(h,g)=∑kmin⁡(hk,gk)K(h, g) = \sum_k \min(h_k, g_k)K(h,g)=∑k​min(hk​,gk​), a direct measure of their overlap. It is a valid kernel on non-negative histograms, and the proof is one line: min⁡(a,b)=∫0∞1{t<a} 1{t<b} dt\min(a, b) = \int_0^\infty 1\{t < a\}\, 1\{t < b\}\, dtmin(a,b)=∫0∞​1{t<a}1{t<b}dt, an inner product of two indicator functions. The generator’s kernel matrix on twenty random histograms has, as it must, no negative eigenvalue. The general lesson is the recipe of the kernel trick read backwards: for an unusual kind of input, ask what similarity you would want between two examples, then make sure it is an inner product in some space, and the SVM machinery applies unchanged.

2  Linear Regression and Gradient Descent

Lecture 2

Keywords

linear regression, gradient descent, stochastic gradient descent, least squares, normal equations, LMS update

Lecture video: Lecture 2: Linear Regression and Gradient Descent · 1:18:17

Based on the lecture by Andrew Ng — CS229, Stanford University, Autumn 2018.

The second lecture builds the first complete learning algorithm of the course and then solves the same problem a second way. Linear regression predicts a number as a weighted sum of features; the weights are chosen to make the squared error on the training set as small as possible; and that minimum can be found either by walking downhill on the error surface, one gradient step at a time, or by solving a linear system for it directly. Gradient descent is the half that matters later: the normal equations work for linear regression and its weighted variant and nothing else in the course, while the walk downhill reappears in logistic regression, generalised linear models and neural networks. Ng uses this lecture to set up the vocabulary all of them share — hypothesis, parameters, cost function, learning rate — and it is worth getting that vocabulary straight here, where the mathematics is still simple enough to check by hand.

2.1 The housing problem and the hypothesis

1:55 The running example is the one that closed lecture 1: predict the asking price of a house from its size. Ng’s data are real, a few dozen listings from Portland, Oregon collected off Craigslist — a 2,104 ft² house listed at $400,000, a 1,416 ft² one at $232,000, and so on — and the task is to fit a straight line through them.

Before writing any formula he describes the shape every supervised algorithm in the course will have. A training set goes into a learning algorithm, and what comes out is a function, by convention called the hypothesis hhh, which takes the size of a house it has never seen and returns an estimated price. The learning algorithm’s job is to produce hhh; hhh’s job is to make predictions. 4:43 The first design decision is therefore not how to fit, but how to represent hhh, and for linear regression the answer is

hθ(x)=θ0+θ1x1+θ2x2,(2.1) h_\theta(x) = \theta_0 + \theta_1 x_1 + \theta_2 x_2, \tag{2.1}hθ​(x)=θ0​+θ1​x1​+θ2​x2​,(2.1)

with x1x_1x1​ the size and x2x_2x2​ the number of bedrooms. Ng concedes the point a mathematician in the room would raise: with the θ0\theta_0θ0​ term this is an affine function, not a linear one, and machine learning calls it linear anyway.

The intercept is awkward to carry around, so it is absorbed into the sum by inventing a feature that is always one, x0=1x_0 = 1x0​=1. Then xxx and θ\thetaθ are both vectors in Rn+1\mathbb{R}^{n+1}Rn+1, and

hθ(x)=∑j=0nθjxj=θ⊤x.(2.2) h_\theta(x) = \sum_{j=0}^{n} \theta_j x_j = \theta^\top x . \tag{2.2}hθ​(x)=j=0∑n​θj​xj​=θ⊤x.(2.2)

The rest of the notation is fixed here for the whole quarter. The θj\theta_jθj​ are the parameters, and choosing them is all that learning means for this model. mmm is the number of training examples (rows of the table), nnn the number of features (two here, so xxx and θ\thetaθ are three-dimensional), xxx the input features and yyy the output or target. The iii-th training example is written (x(i),y(i))(x^{(i)}, y^{(i)})(x(i),y(i)), and the superscript in parentheses is an index into the table, not a power: x1(1)=2104x^{(1)}_1 = 2104x1(1)​=2104 is the size of the first house. Ng is open that some of this terminology is an accident of history — he does not think “hypothesis” is a good name, but someone used it in a paper decades ago and the field is stuck with it.

2.2 The least-squares cost

14:41 “Choose θ\thetaθ so that h(x)h(x)h(x) is close to yyy on the training examples” needs a number that measures closeness, and Ng builds it one piece at a time on the board: the squared difference between prediction and truth for one house, summed over all mmm houses, with a factor of one half in front:

J(θ)=12∑i=1m(hθ(x(i))−y(i))2.(2.3) J(\theta) = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \left( h_\theta(x^{(i)}) - y^{(i)} \right)^2 . \tag{2.3}J(θ)=21​i=1∑m​(hθ​(x(i))−y(i))2.(2.3)

JJJ is the cost function, and linear regression — ordinary least squares — is the choice of θ\thetaθ that minimises it. The one half changes nothing about where the minimum is; it is there so that the 2 brought down by differentiating a square cancels it, as it will in Section 2.4.

16:39 The question Ng says he is asked most often is why the error is squared rather than taken in absolute value or raised to the fourth power. His answer here is a deferral with a hint: squared error corresponds to assuming the noise in yyy is Gaussian, and linear regression turns out to be one member of a family, the generalised linear models, in which that choice is derived rather than assumed. The next lecture makes the Gaussian argument precise.

2.3 Gradient descent

18:27 The algorithm for minimising JJJ starts from some initial θ\thetaθ — the zero vector is a reasonable default, a random start is also fine — and keeps changing θ\thetaθ in a way that lowers JJJ.

19:40 Ng’s picture for it is a landscape. Put θ0\theta_0θ0​ and θ1\theta_1θ1​ on the two horizontal axes and JJJ as the height (with three parameters the surface lives in four dimensions, which is why the picture uses two). Stand at the starting point, turn through 360 degrees, and ask: if I took one tiny step, which direction would take me downhill fastest? Take that step, look around again from the new spot, and repeat.

22:03 The picture also exposes the method’s weakness. On a landscape with more than one valley, where you end up depends on where you start: begin a few steps to one side and the same procedure walks you into a different local minimum (Figure 2.1). For linear regression this turns out not to matter, for a reason Section 2.5 gives, but it matters a great deal for most of what comes later in the course.

23:54 Formally, one step of gradient descent updates every parameter at once:

θj:=θj−α∂∂θjJ(θ),j=0,1,…,n.(2.4) \theta_j := \theta_j - \alpha \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta_j} J(\theta), \qquad j = 0, 1, \dots, n . \tag{2.4}θj​:=θj​−α∂θj​∂​J(θ),j=0,1,…,n.(2.4)

The partial derivative gives the direction of steepest ascent, so subtracting a multiple of it goes downhill. The multiple α\alphaα is the learning rate, which sets the size of the step. The := is assignment, and Ng is deliberate about it: a:=a+1a := a + 1a:=a+1 increments aaa, whereas a=ba = ba=b asserts a fact, and he promises never to write a=a+1a = a + 1a=a+1. Throughout the update, the training set is fixed and JJJ is a fixed function of θ\thetaθ; only θ\thetaθ moves.

26:04 A student asked at once how to choose α\alphaα. “There’s a theory and there’s a practice,” and the practice, to laughter, is 0.010.010.01. The serious version followed: if the features are scaled to roughly [−1,1][-1, 1][−1,1], start at 0.010.010.01 and try larger and smaller values to see which drives JJJ down fastest. Section 2.5 says why the scaling clause is not optional.

2.4 The LMS update

27:27 To run Equation 2.4 the partial derivative has to be computed, and Ng does it for a training set of a single example (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) first, so that the sum does not get in the way. The chain rule brings the 2 down from the square, where it cancels the half, and leaves the derivative of what was inside:

∂∂θj12(hθ(x)−y)2=(hθ(x)−y)∂∂θj(θ0x0+θ1x1+⋯+θnxn−y)=(hθ(x)−y)xj.(2.5) \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta_j} \frac{1}{2}\left(h_\theta(x) - y\right)^2 = \left(h_\theta(x) - y\right) \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta_j} \left( \theta_0 x_0 + \theta_1 x_1 + \dots + \theta_n x_n - y \right) = \left(h_\theta(x) - y\right) x_j . \tag{2.5}∂θj​∂​21​(hθ​(x)−y)2=(hθ​(x)−y)∂θj​∂​(θ0​x0​+θ1​x1​+⋯+θn​xn​−y)=(hθ​(x)−y)xj​.(2.5)

Of all the terms in the expanded sum only θjxj\theta_j x_jθj​xj​ depends on θj\theta_jθj​, so every other term differentiates to zero and this one to xjx_jxj​.

31:43 With mmm examples, the derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives, and the full algorithm is

repeat until convergence:θj:=θj−α∑i=1m(hθ(x(i))−y(i))xj(i)for every j.(2.6) \text{repeat until convergence:} \quad \theta_j := \theta_j - \alpha \sum_{i=1}^{m} \left( h_\theta(x^{(i)}) - y^{(i)} \right) x_j^{(i)} \quad \text{for every } j . \tag{2.6}repeat until convergence:θj​:=θj​−αi=1∑m​(hθ​(x(i))−y(i))xj(i)​for every j.(2.6)

This is the least-mean-squares or Widrow–Hoff rule, and it reads well once written out. Each example pushes θj\theta_jθj​ in proportion to its own error: a house whose price is already predicted almost exactly barely moves the parameters, one that is badly mispredicted moves them a lot, and the push on θj\theta_jθj​ is weighted by how large the feature xjx_jxj​ was for that house. The generator behind this chapter checks Equation 2.6’s sum against finite differences of JJJ on the housing data, and it agrees.

2.5 A bowl, and the learning rate

33:52 The local-minimum worry of Figure 2.1 disappears for this cost. JJJ is a sum of squares of functions linear in θ\thetaθ, so it is a quadratic in θ\thetaθ, and a quadratic of this kind is a single bowl: its only local minimum is the global one. Looked at from above, its contours are nested ellipses, and Ng adds a fact he half-remembers from school — “I think it’s true” — that each step of steepest descent crosses the contour it starts on at right angles. It is true: the gradient is normal to the level set through any point, and the generator confirms it on the housing cost to within a cosine of 0.003.

What the board picture does not show is how thin the bowl can be. Figure 2.2 runs gradient descent on synthetic housing data shaped like Ng’s, with size in thousands of square feet, starting from θ=0\theta = 0θ=0. The first step drops almost straight across the contours into the floor of a long valley; every step after that inches along the floor towards the minimum, and it takes more than four hundred of them to bring the intercept within $500 of its final value. The ratio of the bowl’s steepest to its shallowest curvature — the eigenvalues of X⊤XX^\top XX⊤X, in the notation of Section 2.8 — is about a hundred here, and gradient descent’s pace is set by the shallow direction while its step size is capped by the steep one.

That cap is the precise version of Ng’s 36:08 advice on the learning rate. Too small and the algorithm needs very many iterations; too large and each step overshoots the minimum, lands higher on the far side, and diverges (Figure 2.3). For a quadratic the boundary is exact: with λmax⁡\lambda_{\max}λmax​ the largest eigenvalue of X⊤XX^\top XX⊤X, gradient descent converges for α<2/λmax⁡\alpha < 2/\lambda_{\max}α<2/λmax​ and diverges above it, which the generator checks on both sides of the line. His practical rules follow from watching JJJ rather than computing eigenvalues. If JJJ goes up over iterations, the learning rate is almost certainly too large. And rather than search finely, try values on a roughly doubling scale — 0.01, 0.02, 0.04, 0.08 — and keep whichever drives JJJ down fastest.

The same bound explains the feature-scaling clause in “set it to 0.01”. On the synthetic data with size measured in raw square feet, λmax⁡\lambda_{\max}λmax​ is so large that the stable range ends at α≈4×10−9\alpha \approx 4 \times 10^{-9}α≈4×10−9, and 0.010.010.01 diverges immediately; with size rescaled to about [−1,1][-1, 1][−1,1] the limit is about 0.040.040.04, and 0.010.010.01 is comfortably safe. One further caveat follows from writing JJJ as a sum. λmax⁡\lambda_{\max}λmax​ grows with mmm, so the safe learning rate shrinks as the training set grows, and 0.010.010.01 that works on 47 houses will not on 47,000. Many implementations average the squared error instead of summing it, which removes that dependence; the minimum is unchanged either way.

37:14 Ng’s second view of the same run is in the data rather than in parameter space. With θ=0\theta = 0θ=0 the hypothesis is the horizontal line h(x)=0h(x) = 0h(x)=0, predicting every house free; each step of gradient descent replaces it with a line that fits a little better, until it settles on the least-squares fit (Figure 2.4).

39:30 A student asked why the update subtracts α\alphaα times the gradient rather than adding it. Ng’s suggestion was to work one case by hand, and it settles the matter: take J(θ)=θ2J(\theta) = \theta^2J(θ)=θ2 at θ=1\theta = 1θ=1. The derivative there is 222 (Ng said 1 at the board; the sign is what matters), positive, and to reach the minimum at zero θ\thetaθ has to decrease — so the step must go against the gradient. Adding it would climb.

2.6 Stochastic gradient descent

41:05 Equation 2.6 is called batch gradient descent because every single update looks at the whole training set as one batch. That is its weakness. If mmm is the size of the US census rather than of a Craigslist scrape — Ng mentions training on hundreds of millions of examples — then one step requires a full pass over the data, possibly terabytes read from disk, before θ\thetaθ moves at all, and convergence can take hundreds or thousands of such steps.

44:13 Stochastic gradient descent updates after every example instead:

loop: for i=1,…,m:θj:=θj−α(hθ(x(i))−y(i))xj(i)for every j.(2.7) \text{loop: for } i = 1, \dots, m: \quad \theta_j := \theta_j - \alpha \left( h_\theta(x^{(i)}) - y^{(i)} \right) x_j^{(i)} \quad \text{for every } j . \tag{2.7}loop: for i=1,…,m:θj​:=θj​−α(hθ​(x(i))−y(i))xj(i)​for every j.(2.7)

46:02 In Ng’s words it looks at one house and adjusts the parameters to predict that house better, then the next house, then the next. No single house’s gradient points straight at the minimum, so the path is noisy, but on average it heads downhill, and it starts making progress after the first example rather than after the millionth. The price is that it never quite converges: because each update chases a different house, θ\thetaθ keeps wandering around the minimum, as on the right of Figure 2.2. For a large dataset that trade is almost always worth making.

The questions from the room fill in how it is used in practice. Asked 47:58 whether one could start stochastic and switch to batch near the end, Ng said yes, and that it does no harm — but that people rarely bother, because batch steps on a large dataset are too slow to be worth it, and because the parameters stochastic gradient descent ends near are usually perfectly good even if they are not exactly the global minimum. The middle ground, which he names and leaves to CS230, is mini-batch gradient descent, which updates on a hundred or so examples at a time. What is common instead is 50:03 to decrease the learning rate slowly over time: the oscillations shrink with the step size, so θ\thetaθ ends up bouncing around a smaller region closer to the minimum. Asked when to stop, his answer was to plot JJJ over time and stop when it stops going down — easy here, where there are no local optima to confuse the picture, and much harder for the neural networks later in the course.

51:35 The last question was whether stochastic gradient descent’s learning rate should be 1/m1/m1/m times batch’s. No, Ng said, it is usually much bigger, since otherwise it would be as slow as batch. The arithmetic behind that answer is worth having. Because JJJ is a sum, a full pass of Equation 2.7 at rate α\alphaα adds up, to first order in α\alphaα, to one batch step at the same α\alphaα; the generator confirms the gap between them shrinking as α\alphaα does. Dividing by mmm would therefore make a whole pass of stochastic updates worth only 1/m1/m1/m of a batch step. And a single-example update tolerates a far larger rate in the first place: its stability limit is 2/∥x(i)∥22 / \lVert x^{(i)} \rVert^22/∥x(i)∥2 rather than 2/λmax⁡2 / \lambda_{\max}2/λmax​ of the whole of X⊤XX^\top XX⊤X, which on the synthetic housing data is a factor of about 25 more room.

51:51 Ng’s own rule of thumb closes the topic. With a dataset of hundreds or thousands of examples, where a pass is cheap, he would almost always use batch gradient descent, because it is one less thing to fiddle with — no oscillating parameters to worry about. When the data are too large for that, almost everyone uses stochastic gradient descent or something like it.

2.7 Matrix derivatives and the trace

53:25 For linear regression, and for exactly linear regression — none of the models of the following lectures beyond its weighted variant in lecture 3 — there is a way to skip the iterations altogether and jump to the minimum in one step. Deriving it means setting the gradient of JJJ to zero and solving, and Ng’s aim is to do that “in roughly four lines of linear algebra rather than pages and pages”, which takes a little notation first. In his experience, he says, the right notation is what makes such problems tractable.

56:02 For a function J:Rn+1→RJ : \mathbb{R}^{n+1} \to \mathbb{R}J:Rn+1→R, the derivative with respect to the vector θ\thetaθ is the vector of partial derivatives,

∇θJ=[∂J/∂θ0⋮∂J/∂θn],(2.8) \nabla_\theta J = \begin{bmatrix} \partial J / \partial \theta_0 \\ \vdots \\ \partial J / \partial \theta_n \end{bmatrix} , \tag{2.8}∇θ​J=​∂J/∂θ0​⋮∂J/∂θn​​​,(2.8)

and more generally, for a function fff that maps an m×nm \times nm×n matrix AAA to a real number, ∇Af(A)\nabla_A f(A)∇A​f(A) is the m×nm \times nm×n matrix whose (i,j)(i, j)(i,j) entry is ∂f/∂Aij\partial f / \partial A_{ij}∂f/∂Aij​. Ng’s example 58:32 is a made-up function of a 2×22 \times 22×2 matrix, f(A)=A11+A122f(A) = A_{11} + A_{12}^2f(A)=A11​+A122​, so that fff applied to the matrix with rows (5,6)(5, 6)(5,6) and (7,8)(7, 8)(7,8) is 5+36=415 + 36 = 415+36=41, and

∇Af(A)=[12A1200].(2.9) \nabla_A f(A) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2A_{12} \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} . \tag{2.9}∇A​f(A)=[10​2A12​0​].(2.9)

A student asked whether the vector definition was a special case of the matrix one; it is — a vector is an (n+1)×1(n+1) \times 1(n+1)×1 matrix, and the two agree.

1:03:35 The second tool is the trace of a square matrix, the sum of its diagonal entries, tr⁡A=∑iAii\operatorname{tr} A = \sum_i A_{ii}trA=∑i​Aii​. Transposing a matrix leaves its diagonal where it was, so tr⁡A=tr⁡A⊤\operatorname{tr} A = \operatorname{tr} A^\toptrA=trA⊤. The trace of a product is unchanged by moving the last factor to the front, tr⁡AB=tr⁡BA\operatorname{tr} AB = \operatorname{tr} BAtrAB=trBA and tr⁡ABC=tr⁡CAB\operatorname{tr} ABC = \operatorname{tr} CABtrABC=trCAB, and its derivative has a clean form:

∇Atr⁡AB=B⊤.(2.10) \nabla_A \operatorname{tr} AB = B^\top . \tag{2.10}∇A​trAB=B⊤.(2.10)

1:06:23 Ng then gives one identity as the matrix analogue of ddaa2c=2ac\frac{d}{da} a^2 c = 2acdad​a2c=2ac, a rule to be used without re-deriving it every time, the way one uses the derivative of x2x^2x2. The analogy is a good way to remember the shape of the result, but the generator caught that the shorthand needs a condition the board left out:

∇Atr⁡ ⁣(AA⊤C)=(C+C⊤)A,(2.11) \nabla_A \operatorname{tr}\!\left(A A^\top C\right) = \left(C + C^\top\right) A , \tag{2.11}∇A​tr(AA⊤C)=(C+C⊤)A,(2.11)

which equals 2CA2CA2CA only when CCC is symmetric. For a random non-symmetric CCC, the numerical gradient matches Equation 2.11 and misses 2CA2CA2CA by a visible margin; for the symmetric part of the same CCC the two coincide. In the derivation that follows AAA is θ\thetaθ and the matrix playing the role of CCC is X⊤XX^\top XX⊤X, which is symmetric, so nothing goes wrong, but the general rule is the one with C+C⊤C + C^\topC+C⊤. The official notes state the fully general version, ∇Atr⁡ABA⊤C=CAB+C⊤AB⊤\nabla_A \operatorname{tr} ABA^\top C = CAB + C^\top A B^\top∇A​trABA⊤C=CAB+C⊤AB⊤, which reduces to Equation 2.11 when BBB is the identity.

2.8 The normal equations

1:08:39 To write JJJ with matrices, stack the training inputs as the rows of the design matrix XXX, an m×(n+1)m \times (n+1)m×(n+1) matrix whose iii-th row is (x(i))⊤(x^{(i)})^\top(x(i))⊤, and stack the targets into a vector y⃗∈Rm\vec{y} \in \mathbb{R}^my​∈Rm. Then XθX\thetaXθ is the vector of every prediction at once, since its iii-th entry is (x(i))⊤θ=hθ(x(i))(x^{(i)})^\top \theta = h_\theta(x^{(i)})(x(i))⊤θ=hθ​(x(i)), and Xθ−y⃗X\theta - \vec{y}Xθ−y​ is the vector of every error. A vector dotted with itself is the sum of its squared entries, z⊤z=∑izi2z^\top z = \sum_i z_i^2z⊤z=∑i​zi2​, so

J(θ)=12(Xθ−y⃗)⊤(Xθ−y⃗).(2.12) J(\theta) = \frac{1}{2} \left( X\theta - \vec{y} \right)^\top \left( X\theta - \vec{y} \right) . \tag{2.12}J(θ)=21​(Xθ−y​)⊤(Xθ−y​).(2.12)

1:13:08 Now take the gradient and set it to zero. Expanding the product the way one expands (ax−b)2(ax - b)^2(ax−b)2 gives four terms, θ⊤X⊤Xθ−θ⊤X⊤y⃗−y⃗⊤Xθ+y⃗⊤y⃗\theta^\top X^\top X \theta - \theta^\top X^\top \vec{y} - \vec{y}^\top X \theta + \vec{y}^\top \vec{y}θ⊤X⊤Xθ−θ⊤X⊤y​−y​⊤Xθ+y​⊤y​. The last does not depend on θ\thetaθ; the middle two are the same scalar, each the transpose of the other; and the trace rules of Section 2.7 differentiate the first to 2X⊤Xθ2X^\top X\theta2X⊤Xθ and the middle pair to 2X⊤y⃗2X^\top\vec{y}2X⊤y​. Halving,

∇θJ(θ)=X⊤Xθ−X⊤y⃗.(2.13) \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = X^\top X \theta - X^\top \vec{y} . \tag{2.13}∇θ​J(θ)=X⊤Xθ−X⊤y​.(2.13)

This is Equation 2.6’s sum written in matrix form, which is a useful check on both. 1:16:19 Setting it to zero gives the normal equations,

X⊤Xθ=X⊤y⃗,θ=(X⊤X)−1X⊤y⃗.(2.14) X^\top X \theta = X^\top \vec{y} , \qquad \theta = \left( X^\top X \right)^{-1} X^\top \vec{y} . \tag{2.14}X⊤Xθ=X⊤y​,θ=(X⊤X)−1X⊤y​.(2.14)

A few matrix products land on the global minimum of the bowl in one step, the same point that gradient descent spends hundreds of steps approaching in Figure 2.2; the generator runs both and confirms they agree to six decimal places. On the real Portland data the official notes report the fit θ0=71.27\theta_0 = 71.27θ0​=71.27, θ1=0.1345\theta_1 = 0.1345θ1​=0.1345: about $71,000 plus $134.50 per square foot.

1:17:01 The common question is what to do if X⊤XX^\top XX⊤X is not invertible. That usually means the features are linearly dependent — redundant — and the matrix pseudo-inverse will still return a sensible answer. But Ng’s preferred fix is diagnostic: a singular X⊤XX^\top XX⊤X most often means the same feature is in the data twice (size in square feet and size in square metres, say), and the right move is to find the duplicate and drop it. With a feature duplicated, every way of splitting its weight between the two copies gives exactly the same predictions and the same JJJ, which is why no single θ\thetaθ can be picked out by the equations; the generator checks this on a copy of the housing data with the size column repeated.

His last answer, to a final question on how to choose the learning rate, came back to where Section 2.3 left it: it is empirical, and most people try several values and keep the one that works.

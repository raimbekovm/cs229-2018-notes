19  Finite-Horizon MDPs and the Linear Quadratic Regulator

Lecture 19

Keywords

finite-horizon MDPs, linear quadratic regulator, LQR, Riccati equation, linearization

Lecture video: Lecture 19: Reward Model and Linear Dynamical System · 1:21:07

Based on the lecture by Andrew Ng — CS229, Stanford University, Autumn 2018.

Fitted value iteration (Section 18.4) handles continuous states by approximating the value function. This lecture finds the one important case where no approximation is needed: if the dynamics are linear and the reward quadratic, the value function is exactly quadratic, the optimal policy is exactly linear, and both come out of a short matrix recursion however many state variables there are. Getting there takes two small generalizations of the MDP first, rewards that depend on the action and a finite horizon, because the algorithm, the linear quadratic regulator (LQR), is most naturally a backward pass over a fixed number of time steps. Ng’s summary is that LQR applies to a narrow set of problems, but when it applies it is the algorithm to use.

19.1 Rewards that depend on the action

4:16 So far the reward has been a function of the state alone. Letting it depend on the action too, R:S×A→RR : S \times A \to \mathbb{R}R:S×A→R, makes the payoff

R(s0,a0)+γR(s1,a1)+γ2R(s2,a2)+⋯ , R(s_0, a_0) + \gamma R(s_1, a_1) + \gamma^2 R(s_2, a_2) + \cdots, R(s0​,a0​)+γR(s1​,a1​)+γ2R(s2​,a2​)+⋯,

and it lets a problem say that actions have costs. A robot in a maze can be charged for moving and nothing for standing still. A helicopter can be charged for yanking the sticks hard, which makes it jerk around. A route planner can charge each road segment its own cost, and a factory can price sending a maintenance crew differently from doing nothing. 5:55 Bellman’s equation changes in one visible way:

V∗(s)=max⁡a[R(s,a)+γ∑s′Psa(s′)V∗(s′)],(19.1) V^*(s) = \max_a \Bigl[ R(s, a) + \gamma \sum_{s'} P_{sa}(s') V^*(s') \Bigr], \tag{19.1}V∗(s)=amax​[R(s,a)+γs′∑​Psa​(s′)V∗(s′)],(19.1)

with the max now outside the immediate reward, since that reward depends on the action being chosen. A student checks that the max covers the whole bracket, and it does. Value iteration uses Equation 19.1 as its update unchanged, and the optimal policy is the argmax of the same bracket. The reward depends on the current state and action, not on the next state. Ng notes that other formulations exist and this is one convenient one.

19.2 Finite horizons and policies that watch the clock

11:00 The second generalization replaces the discount factor with a horizon TTT. The MDP runs for TTT steps and then stops, and the payoff is the finite sum

R(s0,a0)+R(s1,a1)+⋯+R(sT,aT), R(s_0, a_0) + R(s_1, a_1) + \cdots + R(s_T, a_T), R(s0​,a0​)+R(s1​,a1​)+⋯+R(sT​,aT​),

usually without discounting, though discounting is still allowed. Ng’s example is a model helicopter with thirty minutes of fuel: the aim is to collect as much reward as possible before the clock runs out.

13:11 A finite horizon has a consequence that is easy to miss. The best action can depend on the time. Put a robot between a small +1+1+1 nearby and a large +10+10+10 farther off. With plenty of time it should go for the +10+10+10. With two or three steps left it should grab the +1+1+1, because the +10+10+10 is out of reach. So the optimal policy is written πt∗(s)\pi^*_t(s)πt∗​(s), and is called non-stationary. 15:46 Ng points out what was implicit until now: in the infinite-horizon discounted setting of lectures 16 and 17 the optimal policy is stationary, the same at every step, because the future always looks the same.

16:31 Once the policy may change over time, so may the world. A non-stationary MDP has Psa(t)P^{(t)}_{sa}Psa(t)​ and R(t)(s,a)R^{(t)}(s, a)R(t)(s,a) indexed by time. Ng gives three examples. A long-haul airliner can burn a third of its take-off weight in fuel, so it handles differently on landing. A drive to San Francisco at 5 pm runs into rush hour, and into mud if the forecast says rain. And in a factory running round the clock, labour costs and availability change with the shift.

19.3 Solving it backwards

21:11 Define Vt∗(s)V^*_t(s)Vt∗​(s) as the best expected payoff from state sss at time ttt to the end. With nothing left after the last step, the base case is just the best immediate reward,

VT∗(s)=max⁡aR(s,a), V^*_T(s) = \max_a R(s, a), VT∗​(s)=amax​R(s,a),

Vt∗(s)=max⁡a[R(s,a)+∑s′Psa(s′)Vt+1∗(s′)],πt∗(s)=arg⁡max⁡a[⋯ ].(19.2) V^*_t(s) = \max_a \Bigl[ R(s, a) + \sum_{s'} P_{sa}(s') V^*_{t+1}(s') \Bigr], \qquad \pi^*_t(s) = \arg\max_a \Bigl[ \cdots \Bigr]. \tag{19.2}Vt∗​(s)=amax​[R(s,a)+s′∑​Psa​(s′)Vt+1∗​(s′)],πt∗​(s)=argamax​[⋯].(19.2)

This is no longer value iteration in the sense of repeating an update until it converges. It is dynamic programming: compute VT∗V^*_TVT∗​, then VT−1∗V^*_{T-1}VT−1∗​ from it, then VT−2∗V^*_{T-2}VT−2∗​, down to V0∗V^*_0V0∗​, once each, and read πt∗\pi^*_tπt∗​ off at every step. Time-varying P(t)P^{(t)}P(t) and R(t)R^{(t)}R(t) slot into Equation 19.2 at no extra cost.

28:31 A student asks whether letting T→∞T \to \inftyT→∞ recovers ordinary value iteration. The two are closely related, but undiscounted sums can grow without bound as TTT grows. That is one reason discounting exists. If ∣R∣≤Rmax⁡|R| \le R_{\max}∣R∣≤Rmax​, a discounted value is at most Rmax⁡/(1−γ)R_{\max}/(1-\gamma)Rmax​/(1−γ) in size, and a finite-horizon one at most (T+1)Rmax⁡(T+1) R_{\max}(T+1)Rmax​.

30:30 Ng sketches the +1+1+1/+10+10+10 corridor with deterministic moves. With two steps left, the values read like “10, 10, 10, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 0”: cells within reach of the +10+10+10, cells only within reach of the +1+1+1, and cells that can reach neither. He suggests building the little simulator to watch the policy change as the clock runs down. Figure 19.1 does that for an eleven-cell corridor of our own, with the +10+10+10 in cell 0, the +1+1+1 in cell 8, and a state-action reward of −0.1-0.1−0.1 for each move, so that it uses both of this lecture’s generalizations. The generator checks the dynamic programme against brute force over every action sequence up to seven steps. Cell 5 shows the non-stationarity in full. With one or two steps left it stays put, since nothing is reachable and moving costs. With three or four it heads right for the +1+1+1. With five or more it heads left for the +10+10+10.

The figure’s caption hides one more lesson. Without discounting and with waiting free, many cells have several optimal actions. Leaving for the +10+10+10 now or one step later gives the same total if there is time either way. Discounting is what makes sooner strictly better.

19.4 The LQR model

32:04 LQR is a finite-horizon MDP with continuous states S=RnS = \mathbb{R}^nS=Rn, continuous actions A=RdA = \mathbb{R}^dA=Rd, and two specific assumptions. The first is that the dynamics are linear with Gaussian noise:

st+1=Ast+Bat+wt,wt∼N(0,Σw),(19.3) s_{t+1} = A s_t + B a_t + w_t, \qquad w_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma_w), \tag{19.3}st+1​=Ast​+Bat​+wt​,wt​∼N(0,Σw​),(19.3)

with A∈Rn×nA \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}A∈Rn×n and B∈Rn×dB \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}B∈Rn×d. 34:27 Ng apologises for the notation. AAA is now both the set of actions and a matrix, because LQR comes from control theory in electrical and mechanical engineering and reinforcement learning from computer science, and when the two literatures met, their letters clashed. The same thing happens to VVV below. 36:30 The second assumption is a quadratic reward,

R(s,a)=−(s⊤Us+a⊤Va),U⪰0,  V⪰0,(19.4) R(s, a) = -\bigl(s^\top U s + a^\top V a\bigr), \qquad U \succeq 0,\; V \succeq 0, \tag{19.4}R(s,a)=−(s⊤Us+a⊤Va),U⪰0,V⪰0,(19.4)

with U∈Rn×nU \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}U∈Rn×n and V∈Rd×dV \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}V∈Rd×d positive semi-definite, so both terms are costs. For a helicopter that should hover at the origin, U=IU = IU=I and V=IV = IV=I give R=−∥s∥2−∥a∥2R = -\lVert s \rVert^2 - \lVert a \rVert^2R=−∥s∥2−∥a∥2. That penalises drifting from the hover point and penalises large control inputs. Time-varying AtA_tAt​, BtB_tBt​, UtU_tUt​, VtV_tVt​ are allowed but not needed for the derivation.

40:43 Where do AAA and BBB come from? The first way was lecture 18’s (Equation 18.1): fly the helicopter, log mmm trajectories, and fit AAA and BBB by least squares. Ng says again that this gives a good model for slow flight.

19.5 Linearizing a nonlinear model

43:02 The second way starts from a nonlinear simulator st+1=f(st,at)s_{t+1} = f(s_t, a_t)st+1​=f(st​,at​), from a physics engine or “a friend with a physics degree”. Ng tried the inverted pendulum’s equations himself and found them complicated. To make it linear, he draws the one-dimensional case. 45:31 Plot st+1=f(st)s_{t+1} = f(s_t)st+1​=f(st​) against sts_tst​, pick a point sˉt\bar s_tsˉt​, and replace fff with its tangent line there:

st+1≈f(sˉt)+f′(sˉt) (st−sˉt). s_{t+1} \approx f(\bar s_t) + f'(\bar s_t)\,(s_t - \bar s_t). st+1​≈f(sˉt​)+f′(sˉt​)(st​−sˉt​).

49:05 The tangent is good near sˉt\bar s_tsˉt​ and poor far away, so sˉt\bar s_tsˉt​ should be where the system spends its time: the hover point for a helicopter, the upright pole at rest for the pendulum. 50:37 With an action as well, linearize around a typical pair (sˉt,aˉt)(\bar s_t, \bar a_t)(sˉt​,aˉt​) using the Jacobians:

st+1≈f(sˉt,aˉt)+∇sf(sˉt,aˉt) (st−sˉt)+∇af(sˉt,aˉt) (at−aˉt).(19.5) s_{t+1} \approx f(\bar s_t, \bar a_t) + \nabla_s f(\bar s_t, \bar a_t)\,(s_t - \bar s_t) + \nabla_a f(\bar s_t, \bar a_t)\,(a_t - \bar a_t). \tag{19.5}st+1​≈f(sˉt​,aˉt​)+∇s​f(sˉt​,aˉt​)(st​−sˉt​)+∇a​f(sˉt​,aˉt​)(at​−aˉt​).(19.5)

53:20 This is affine rather than linear, because of the constant terms. Ng’s fix is to add an intercept to the state, a component fixed at 1. Then Equation 19.5 takes the form st+1=Ast+Bats_{t+1} = A s_t + B a_tst+1​=Ast​+Bat​ after “some algebraic munging”. When the linearization point is the origin and the system rests there, f(0,0)=0f(0, 0) = 0f(0,0)=0, as for the upright pendulum, the constants vanish and no intercept is needed.

Figure 19.2 does this for a pendulum on a motorised pivot, with the angle θ\thetaθ measured from upright and a torque as the action. The only nonlinear term is gravity, (g/l)sin⁡θ(g/l)\sin\theta(g/l)sinθ, and linearizing at θˉ=0\bar\theta = 0θˉ=0 replaces it with (g/l)θ(g/l)\theta(g/l)θ. The generator checks the hand-derived AAA and BBB against finite differences. The line is 1% too strong at 0.25 rad, 4% at 0.5 rad, 19% at 1 rad and 50% at 1.5 rad. The right panel runs the LQR controller designed on the linear model on the true pendulum. From 0.3 rad the real and predicted trajectories coincide. From 1.4 rad they come apart by up to 0.11 rad, but the controller still brings the pole up. Feedback forgives a model that is wrong in the right direction. Because the measured state is fed back every step, the controller corrects its own model errors as they appear. In this toy, with unlimited torque, it recovers even from 3 rad. A real motor’s torque limit would end that long before.

19.6 The value function is exactly quadratic

56:05 Now run the backward recursion Equation 19.2 on the LQR model. With continuous states, the sum over s′s's′ becomes an expectation over st+1∼N(Ast+Bat,Σw)s_{t+1} \sim \mathcal{N}(A s_t + B a_t, \Sigma_w)st+1​∼N(Ast​+Bat​,Σw​). 57:07 The base case is immediate:

VT∗(sT)=max⁡aT[−sT⊤UsT−aT⊤VaT]=−sT⊤UsT, V^*_T(s_T) = \max_{a_T} \bigl[-s_T^\top U s_T - a_T^\top V a_T\bigr] = -s_T^\top U s_T, VT∗​(sT​)=aT​max​[−sT⊤​UsT​−aT⊤​VaT​]=−sT⊤​UsT​,

19.7 The noise does not change the policy

1:15:22 Ng ends with a property he calls fun, and warns against generalizing. Look at what each quantity depends on. LtL_tLt​ in Equation 19.6 needs Φt+1\Phi_{t+1}Φt+1​ but not Ψt+1\Psi_{t+1}Ψt+1​, which makes sense: adding a constant to every future value cannot change which action is best. Φt\Phi_tΦt​ in Equation 19.7 needs only Φt+1\Phi_{t+1}Φt+1​. 1:17:36 The noise covariance Σw\Sigma_wΣw​ appears in exactly one place, the update for Ψ\PsiΨ. Delete the Ψ\PsiΨ line from the code and the optimal policy is computed perfectly without Σw\Sigma_wΣw​ ever being used. The optimal LQR policy does not depend on the noise. The value does: a helicopter in a gale does worse than one in still air. But the best response to any state is the same in both.

Figure 19.3 shows both effects on the cart. The gains computed with Σw=0\Sigma_w = 0Σw​=0 and with Σw=0.25I\Sigma_w = 0.25 IΣw​=0.25I are identical to the last bit, while Ψ0\Psi_0Ψ0​ goes from 0 to −161.4-161.4−161.4. The figure also shows the finite horizon at work. For the first half of the horizon the gain is flat at L≈(−2.76,−2.51)L \approx (-2.76, -2.51)L≈(−2.76,−2.51), a stationary policy in all but name. Over the last 25 or so steps it relaxes to zero, because there is less and less future in which a correction would pay off. That is the clock-watching of Figure 19.1 in continuous form.

1:19:00 Ng draws two lessons. The narrow one is that, when applying LQR, there is no need to work hard at estimating Σw\Sigma_wΣw​. The broader one is about practice. For most MDPs the noise does change the policy, and the form of the noise matters. But in his experience of building robots, remembering to add noise matters more than getting its size exactly right. Ten per cent more or less rarely matters; a factor of two often does. That is the same advice as lecture 18’s (Section 18.3), now with one case where it can be proved.
